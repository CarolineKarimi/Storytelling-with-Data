I keep landing in rooms where the analysis was right and the room still didn't move — because the chart made someone work for the point instead of handing it to them. This page is that habit made visible: the same data, once shown, once actually told.

A chart that is technically correct can still fail its audience. The data's all there — it's just not saying anything yet.

Every pair on this page starts from the same source numbers. The left panel is what most tools give you by default: every axis, every label, every color a chart type can hold. The right panel is what's left after deciding what the chart is actually for — usually one comparison, one trend, one number someone in the room needs to walk away remembering.

The practice is built on the framework in Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic's Storytelling with Data: choose an effective visual, cut anything that isn't carrying weight, direct attention on purpose, and say the "so what" out loud instead of leaving it for the audience to find.

Case 01 · Focus attention

Email campaign activity

Two survey metrics, tracked quarterly: the response rate (who opened the email) and the completion rate (who finished it). The intended story is simple — completion has held up well even as response rates crept up.

Showing data
  • Two y-axes force constant back-and-forth reading between scales
  • Every point is labeled — eighteen numbers competing for attention
  • Long "Q1-2020" style labels crowd the x-axis
Storytelling with data
  • Both axes removed; the lines carry the shape, labels carry the values
  • Only the first, last, and turning points are labeled
  • Series named directly on the line — no legend to decode
  • Multi-year average added next to each name for instant context
Case 02 · Eliminate clutter

Length of hospital stay after surgery

Share of patients in each age band, by quarter of 2019. The real question buried in this data: is the middle-aged group (25–36) growing as a share of surgical patients?

Showing data
  • The chart and the table repeat each other — pick one
  • Six overlapping colors with a title that doesn't say "in days"
Storytelling with data

Length of stay is unknown for an average of 1.3% of patients each quarter.

  • Table gone — the bars are the labels now
  • 25–36 is the only band colored; it's clearly the one growing
  • Title says exactly what's being measured, in days
Case 03 · Choose an effective visual

Causes of mortality, Crimean War

Florence Nightingale's original "rose diagram" was groundbreaking for 1858, arguing that more soldiers died of preventable disease than of wounds. It's also, honestly, hard to read today. Same data, rebuilt as a bar and a line.

Showing data (rose-style)
  • 24 monthly wedges, each colored independently — nothing to anchor a scan
  • Area is deceptive: doubling a wedge's radius quadruples its area
  • No easy way to compare one month's cause of death to another's
  • Totals first, as a simple bar: disease dwarfs the other two causes
  • Monthly trend below, disease highlighted, wounds and other muted
  • The takeaway a reader needed in 1858 is now legible in five seconds
Case 04 · Focus attention

Survey results, five items

A five-point agreement scale across five survey items. Two audiences want two different reads: who's split by strength of opinion, and — more urgently — who's simply not on board yet.

Showing data
  • Five categories, five colors, no values — reading requires a ruler
  • "Strongly" vs. plain agree/disagree adds precision nobody asked for here
Storytelling with data
Item C stands out: nearly half of respondents are neutral. Worth a follow-up to find out why.
  • Collapsed to disagree / neutral / agree — the decision that actually matters
  • Direct labels replace the axis; grey holds the middle, color marks the edges
Case 05 · Make it obvious

Sales agents vs. target

Two agents, four quarters each, one target line. The interesting comparison isn't the raw dollars — it's who's beating their number and when.

Showing data
  • Sixteen bars of raw dollars — target and achieved, side by side, eight times
  • Target and achieved use equally loud colors, so neither one wins the eye
Storytelling with data
Agent 1 swings hard with the seasons; Agent 2 grows steadily, on-season or off.
  • One bar per quarter now: percent of target, not two raw dollar figures
  • Green clears the target line, warm grey misses it — color does the sorting
Case 06 · Tell a story

Cumulative shareholder return

$100 invested in 2017, tracked against the S&P 500 and a competitive index. This one's already well-designed by most checklists — clean axes, clear legend. It's still missing the "so what."

Showing data
  • Well de-cluttered, clearly labeled axes — a genuinely decent chart
  • ...and still just a chart. It reports; it doesn't argue anything
Storytelling with data
  • Our company in green, benchmarks pushed back to grey
  • Two callouts do the interpreting the axis labels never could
Case 07 · Choose an effective visual

Marketing spend, sales & profit by store

Three dimensions at once: marketing expense (x), sales (y), profit (bubble size), split across two stores. Bubble charts are a last resort — but when a third dimension genuinely matters, this is how to keep it readable.

Showing data
  • Ten bubbles, ten colors — the two stores that actually matter get lost
  • No labels; every value needs a hover to reveal itself
Storytelling with data
Washing machines: high spend, low profit, across both stores. Store 2's computer spend is worth benchmarking against Store 1.
  • Down to two hues — one per store — so the comparison reads instantly
  • Labels sit on the bubbles themselves; no legend hunting required
Case 08 · Choose an effective visual

Minor population & teacher counts, by state

A choropleth map is the reflexive choice for anything with a state code attached — and it's often the weakest one. It's beautiful for "where," almost useless for "how much more."

Showing data (map-style encoding)

Minor population, alphabetical by state — color is the only signal

Teachers, alphabetical by state

  • Alphabetical order (or geography) buries the actual ranking
  • Hue alone can't carry precise comparison — is CO bigger than SC? Squint
Storytelling with data
  • Sorted, not geographic — the ranking is the point
  • Only the extremes are labeled; the shape tells the rest

Field notes

Choosing a visual, in short

The working rules behind every rebuild above — my own condensed notes, not a universal law.

Reach for

  • A number or two on their own, set big — skip the chart entirely
  • Horizontal bars for categorical comparisons, ordered on purpose
  • Line charts for anything continuous over time, at consistent intervals
  • Scatter plots when the relationship between two things is the story

Handle with care

  • Tables — fine for lookup, weak for live presentation; let data labels do the work instead
  • Heat maps — always ship with a low-to-high legend
  • Secondary axes — try direct labeling or splitting the y-axis before reaching for one
  • Stacked bars — add absolute totals so the subcomponents aren't guesswork

Avoid

  • Pie and donut charts — a sorted horizontal bar almost always reads faster
  • 3-D anything — it distorts the exact numbers it's supposed to show
  • Area charts — human eyes are bad at judging 2-D space
  • Choropleth maps for ranking — great for "where," bad for "how much more"
